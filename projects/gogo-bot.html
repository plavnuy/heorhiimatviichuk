GOGO Bot

Format Startup / MVP
Role Product Designer
Status Archived (War in Ukraine)

Idea

The idea was simple: users scroll through a feed of local events and like those they would like to participate in. Users can also create their own events.

When someone likes an event, the creator receives a notification and decides whether to get in touch. Communication only starts after mutual interest, keeping personal contacts private.

Background

My friend and I were tired of working on other people’s products and wanted to build something of our own. He is a backend developer, I am a designer. Together we had around 10 years of experience in IT.

What started as casual brainstorming sessions in cafés evolved into regular calls, shared chats, and lean boards. We interviewed early adopters found through personal networks and social media.

One by one, ideas failed. At the time, we believed users simply had no real problems. Later, we realized the issue was our lack of experience in running deep interviews, forming hypotheses, and identifying true early adopters.

Eventually, we decided to build an MVP anyway — to experience the pain of developing a product nobody needs, firsthand.

Problems

City residents often experience loneliness and boredom — a problem that became even more visible during the pandemic. People want to meet others nearby, but existing tools mostly focus on dating or large public events.

Public chats are overcrowded and uncomfortable, and it is hard to start a conversation without a shared context. When moving to a new city, it is especially difficult to find local events and meaningful connections.

Existing solutions like Couchsurfing Hangouts offer limited engagement, unclear intentions, and low adoption among locals.

Development

We initially planned an iOS app but quickly realized it would take at least six months and require investments. The idea itself was still unproven.

We decided to test it using a Telegram bot — a popular format for MVPs. The functionality was intentionally reduced to the core idea.

Backend development took around two full weeks. The stack included: git, serverless architecture, AWS API Gateway, AWS Lambda, Node.js, MongoDB, and Telegraf. All infrastructure ran on free trial tiers.

Reality hit when we realized we had exactly two users: my friend and myself.

Early Marketing

We were suddenly faced with postponed questions: who are our users, how to attract them, how to monetize, and what to do next.

After initial frustration, we decided to launch anyway and collect feedback. Kyiv was chosen as the starting city due to cultural familiarity and easier access to interviews.

Audience Segments

We roughly segmented users into four groups: youth (16–27), working adults (27–34), travelers and expats, and school students.

For each segment, we described communication channels, monthly budgets, interests, content consumption habits, and event categories.

Metrics

Key metrics included user registrations, event creation, daily views, likes, unique sessions, and churn.

First Users & Insights

The first users came through free promotion in local group chats. People liked events but rarely created their own.

Interviews revealed that many users did not realize event creation was even possible. We redesigned the flow to push creation earlier and simplified the process to under one minute.

Outcome

The project was frozen due to the full-scale war in Ukraine. Possible next steps included paid marketing, expansion to other cities, fundraising, pivoting, or shutting the project down entirely.
